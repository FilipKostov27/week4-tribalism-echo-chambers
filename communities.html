WEEK 4 / PARTS 1 & 2

Shared interests,
strong loyalties

Specialty coffee influencers on YouTube and BookTok on TikTok

I chose these two communities because they show how belonging and influence can develop around ordinary interests. An echo chamber limits the perspectives we encounter, while tribalism involves strong loyalty to a group. Enjoying the same hobby is not enough to establish either problem. The concern begins when loyalty makes disagreement unwelcome or familiar opinions become the only ones we seriously consider.

1. Specialty coffee influencers on YouTube

I focused on the culture surrounding coffee tutorials, equipment reviews, and influential creators. James Hoffmann’s The Ultimate V60 Technique is a concrete example: it offers viewers a shared brewing method to learn and discuss. His video I Drank A $335 (£265) Espresso. How Did It Taste? presents an expensive tasting experience. The titles illustrate two attractions of the community: improving a practical skill and exploring exceptional products.

Personally, I can see why this appeals to people interested in quality and technical details. However, repeatedly encountering the same expert’s methods could make one approach feel like the only legitimate way to enjoy coffee. Expensive equipment can also become a symbol of belonging if viewers start judging one another through their setups. These are possible mechanisms of tribalism, not proof that every follower behaves this way or that these creators encourage exclusion.

There is also evidence of disagreement. In a January 2024 discussion of Lance Hedrick’s YouTube videos, participants shared practical tips, but one questioned how confidently his data could support the conclusions. This is an example of the YouTube discussion continuing on another platform, and it prevents me from describing the entire coffee community as a closed echo chamber. Hoffmann also acknowledges limits to his product experience and directs readers to other forums.

My conclusion: Shared techniques, expert authority, and enthusiasm for equipment can create a strong community identity. The risk is treating a favorite creator’s judgment as unquestionable. Comparing methods, conducting blind tastings, and respecting different budgets would help keep the community open.

2. BookTok on TikTok

BookTok brings readers together through recommendations, reactions, and shared enthusiasm for books. Maddox and Gill’s 2023 study of 150 BookTok videos collected in September 2022 documents how user practices and TikTok’s interface help construct this community. One example involved a creator discussing several authors but using only a Colleen Hoover author hashtag. Another tagged Hoover’s account. The researchers interpret these choices as attempts to gain visibility through an already prominent author.

This stood out to me because repeated visibility can make popularity feel like agreement. In my interpretation, a viewer who keeps encountering enthusiastic recommendations for the same author could assume that almost everyone shares that opinion. Loyalty becomes problematic if criticism of a book is treated as an attack on the people who enjoy it.

BookTok can also encourage reading and help people find others with similar interests. My concern is that familiar books and influential voices may crowd out less visible alternatives. The study provides evidence of community boundaries and visibility strategies; it does not establish that every BookTok user receives an identical feed or refuses opposing reviews.

My conclusion: I would compare positive and critical reviews, explore unfamiliar authors and genres, and read a sample before buying. A popular recommendation should begin my evaluation rather than determine it.

How the reinforcement happens

Algorithms: YouTube identifies viewing history, searches, subscriptions, and feedback as recommendation signals. TikTok likewise describes recommendations shaped by viewing and other interactions. Therefore, repeatedly engaging with a narrow selection of creators or subjects can help sustain similar recommendations. This is a possible feedback loop, not a guarantee that all personalization creates an echo chamber. YouTube Help; TikTok Newsroom.

Content and social dynamics: A confident tutorial, an expensive product, or an emotional book recommendation can attract attention. Shared language and favorite creators can then provide a sense of belonging. My interpretation is that the loop becomes more restrictive when members reward agreement and dismiss criticism without examining it.

Possible effects: These communities can support learning, friendship, and enjoyment. However, they may also encourage unnecessary spending, feelings of inadequacy, or anxiety about having the “wrong” taste. In political spaces, a similar habit of dismissing outsiders could weaken willingness to consider evidence. I am describing a possible broader consequence, not claiming that coffee videos or BookTok caused political polarization.

Strategies: Users can deliberately compare credible disagreements, check commercial disclosures, and use recommendation feedback. Creators can explain uncertainty, welcome corrections, and avoid treating taste or budget as measures of someone’s worth. Platforms should give people understandable controls and assess content diversity alongside engagement.

Research distinguishes documented examples from my interpretation of their possible effects. Historical examples are dated; they are not a live audit of every community member or recommendation.
